Cambridge IGCSE ICT 0417 · Topic 9

Audience

A short topic with two very different halves: knowing who you are creating a solution for, and knowing the law that protects other people's work. Both come up in Paper 1 and both matter in the practical papers.

Syllabus coverage: 9.1 Audience appreciation · 9.2 Copyright
9.1

Audience Appreciation

Showing a clear sense of audience and purpose

The audience is the group of people who will see or use the solution. The purpose is what the solution is meant to achieve - to inform, to persuade, to instruct, to entertain or to sell.

If the solution does not match the audience, it fails however well it is made. A presentation about online safety written for seven-year-olds cannot be written the way it would be for company directors.

  • Every design decision - wording, font, colour, images, length, interactivity - should be justified by the audience and the purpose.
  • The same information may need several different versions for several different audiences.
  • The purpose decides the medium too: a poster for a quick message in a public place, a website for detail, a video for a demonstration.

Analysing the needs of an audience

Before creating an ICT solution, find out:

  • Age - which decides vocabulary, reading level, font size, images and how much text is acceptable
  • Existing knowledge and expertise - whether technical terms can be used, or must be explained or avoided
  • Interests and expectations - what will hold their attention and what tone they expect
  • Language - the languages spoken, and whether a translation or simpler English is required
  • Income and access to technology - whether they have fast internet, a large screen or only a phone
  • Special needs and accessibility - visual impairment, hearing impairment, colour blindness, reading difficulties
  • Cultural background and beliefs - so that images, colours, humour and examples are appropriate and respectful
  • Size of the audience and where they will see the solution - one reader on a phone, or a hall of people watching a projection

How the needs are found out

  • Questionnaires and surveys of the target group
  • Interviews and focus groups
  • Observation of the audience using an existing solution
  • Analysis of existing data, such as website statistics or customer records

Planning solutions that are responsive to and respectful of the audience

Being responsive
  • Match reading level, vocabulary and sentence length to the audience
  • Use a layout that works on the devices they actually use
  • Choose a font size and style that is easy for them to read - larger for young children and older readers
  • Provide alternatives: subtitles on video, alternative text on images, a text version of audio
  • Use interactivity, sound and animation where it helps understanding - not for decoration
  • Test the solution with real members of the audience and act on what they say
Being respectful
  • Avoid language, images or humour that could offend any cultural or religious group
  • Avoid stereotypes, and represent the whole audience fairly
  • Do not use images of people without permission, and respect their privacy
  • Do not patronise the audience or assume a lack of intelligence
  • Consider colour choices carefully - both for meaning in different cultures and for colour-blind readers
  • Be honest: do not exaggerate or mislead
Exam tip: answers must be specific. "Make it suitable for children" earns nothing. "Use a large sans-serif font, short sentences and cartoon images, because the audience is aged 5 to 7 and has limited reading ability" earns marks - a design decision plus a reason.
Check your understanding
A health centre needs a leaflet about flu vaccination for elderly patients. Give three design decisions and justify each.
  • Large, clear sans-serif font with strong contrast, because older readers are more likely to have impaired vision.
  • Short sentences and everyday words rather than medical terms, because the audience are not medical experts.
  • Printed leaflet rather than a website, because some elderly patients may not have internet access or the confidence to use it.
Name two ways of finding out what an audience needs.
Any two of: questionnaires sent to the target group, interviews or focus groups, observing the audience using an existing solution, or analysing existing data such as customer records and website statistics.
9.2

Copyright

The need for copyright legislation

Copyright gives the creator of an original work the legal right to control how it is copied, distributed and used. Software, music, films, books, photographs and web pages are all covered.
  • Software takes a great deal of time, skill and money to produce, and the developers deserve to be paid for it.
  • Without the income from sales, companies could not afford to develop, update and support new software.
  • It protects the creator's ownership of their ideas and their reputation.
  • It gives the creator the legal means to take action against anyone who copies their work.
  • Illegal copies are often altered and may carry malware, harming users as well as developers.

Principles of copyright relating to computer software

  • Software is licensed, not sold - the buyer purchases the right to use it under stated conditions.
  • It is illegal to make a copy of software and sell it, or to give it away.
  • It is illegal to use software on more machines than the licence allows, or to use a single-user licence across a network.
  • It is illegal to use the name of copyrighted software on other software without agreement.
  • It is illegal to rent out software without permission.
  • One copy may normally be made for backup purposes only.
  • Software piracy means copying, distributing or using software illegally - including downloading cracked versions from the internet.

Methods software producers use to prevent copyright being broken

  • A product key or serial number must be entered during installation.
  • The software must be registered and activated online, and the key is checked against the manufacturer's database; the same key cannot be activated many times.
  • The original disc or dongle must be present in the drive or USB port while the software runs.
  • A licence agreement must be accepted before installation, setting out how many machines the software may be used on.
  • Software is supplied as a subscription in the cloud, so a valid account is needed to run it at all.
  • Encryption of the executable, and code that checks for tampering, make cracking harder.
  • Holograms and security features on the packaging help buyers recognise a genuine copy.
  • Updates, technical support and new features are only available to registered users.
Watch the command word: "methods used to prevent copyright being broken" asks for the technical measures above - not for the penalties, and not for a description of what piracy is.
Check your understanding
Give three methods a software producer uses to stop illegal copying.
Any three of: requiring a unique product key at installation; requiring online registration and activation checked against a database; requiring the original disc or a dongle to be present while the software runs; supplying the software as a cloud subscription needing a valid account; making updates and support available only to registered users.
A school buys one copy of a program and installs it on 30 computers. Explain why this is illegal and what it should have done.
The licence bought allows the software to be used on a stated number of machines - usually one - so installing it on 30 breaches the licence agreement and copyright law. The school should have bought a multi-user or site licence covering 30 machines, or a network licence.
