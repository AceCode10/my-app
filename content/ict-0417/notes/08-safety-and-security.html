Cambridge IGCSE ICT 0417 · Topic 8

Safety and Security

Three different ideas sit in this topic and examiners expect you to keep them apart: physical safety (harm to people from the equipment), eSafety (harm to people from what they do online), and security of data (harm to the data itself). Answer the question that was asked.

Syllabus coverage: 8.1 Physical safety · 8.2 eSafety · 8.3 Security of data
8.1

Physical Safety

Safety issues, causes and prevention

Each of the four named safety issues needs a cause and a prevention strategy.

Electrocution
Causes
  • Spilling drinks near electrical equipment
  • Touching live cables with bare or damaged insulation
  • Opening equipment without switching it off first
  • Wet hands or a damp floor near power points
Prevention strategies
  • Do not allow drinks near computers, or use sealed containers well away from equipment
  • Check cables regularly and replace any with damaged insulation
  • Use a residual current device (RCD) in the circuit to cut the power instantly
  • Have equipment checked by a qualified electrician, and switch off before opening a case
  • Keep the area dry, and use insulation and covers on wiring
Fire
Causes
  • Sockets being overloaded - too many devices plugged into one socket
  • Equipment overheating, often because vents are blocked or fans have failed
  • Damaged cables short-circuiting
  • Paper and other combustible material left against equipment
Prevention strategies
  • Do not overload sockets - use one plug per socket and fit enough sockets in the room
  • Keep the room well ventilated and air conditioned, and do not block cooling vents
  • Have a CO2 or dry powder fire extinguisher nearby, never water
  • Fit smoke detectors and check equipment and cabling regularly
  • Keep paper and other combustible materials away from the equipment
Tripping over trailing cables
Causes
  • Power and network cables running across the floor or walkways
  • Too few sockets, so long extension leads are used
  • Untidy cabling behind and between desks
Prevention strategies
  • Use cable ducts, trunking or floor covers to enclose cables
  • Fit more sockets, or supply power from the ceiling, so leads do not cross walkways
  • Use wireless devices and networks to remove cables altogether
  • Tidy cables with clips and ties, and keep walkways clear
Heavy equipment falling and injuring people
Causes
  • Monitors or system units placed on unstable, weak or overcrowded desks
  • Equipment left near the edge of a surface
  • Shelves or wall brackets not strong enough for the weight
Prevention strategies
  • Use strong, large desks designed to take the weight of the equipment
  • Use secure wall brackets for monitors and projectors, fitted professionally
  • Place heavy items well back from the edge, and do not stack equipment
  • Check desks, shelves and brackets regularly
Check your understanding
A computer room has one socket and eight computers. State the safety risk and two ways to remove it.
The risk is fire from an overloaded socket, and there is also a tripping risk from the extension leads. The room should be rewired with enough individual sockets for each machine, and any remaining cables should be enclosed in cable ducts or trunking so they do not cross walkways.
Why should a water extinguisher never be used on a computer fire?
Water conducts electricity, so using it on live electrical equipment risks electrocution and would also destroy the equipment. A CO2 or dry powder extinguisher should be used instead.
8.2

eSafety

Data protection

Why data protection legislation is required

  • Organisations hold huge amounts of personal data about individuals.
  • Without the law, that data could be sold, misused or disclosed without the person's knowledge.
  • Inaccurate data could lead to a person being refused a loan, a job or medical treatment.
  • The law makes organisations legally responsible for keeping data accurate and secure, and gives individuals the right to see and correct data held about them.

Principles of a typical data protection act

  • Data must be fairly and lawfully processed.
  • Data must be processed only for the stated, limited purpose it was collected for.
  • Data must be adequate, relevant and not excessive - only what is needed.
  • Data must be accurate and kept up to date.
  • Data must not be kept longer than necessary.
  • Data must be processed in line with the data subject's rights, including their right to see it.
  • Data must be kept secure, protected against loss, theft and unauthorised access.
  • Data must not be transferred to another country without adequate protection.

Personal and sensitive data

Personal data
  • Data about a living, identifiable individual
  • Personal name, address, date of birth
  • A photograph in school uniform, which shows where the person can be found
  • Email address, phone number, bank details
Sensitive personal data
  • Needs even greater protection
  • Medical history
  • Ethnic origin, religious beliefs, political opinions
  • Criminal record, biometric data

Why personal data must be kept confidential

  • To prevent identity theft and fraud using the person's details.
  • To protect the person's physical safety - a photograph in school uniform, with a name, tells a stranger where to find a child.
  • To prevent discrimination, bullying or embarrassment from inappropriate disclosure.
  • Because the individual has a legal right to privacy.

eSafety - minimising the dangers

Using the internet
  • Only use trusted websites recommended by teachers.
  • Use a search engine that only allows access to age-appropriate websites.
  • Never give out personal details on a website, and check a site is secure (https and the padlock) before entering anything.
  • Be aware that information found online may be inaccurate or biased.
Using email
  • Be aware of the potential dangers of opening or replying to an email from an unknown person.
  • Never open attachments or click links in an email from an unknown sender - they may carry malware or lead to a fake site.
  • Be aware of the risks of sending personal identifiable data or images by email - once sent, you lose control of them.
  • Do not reply to spam, because replying confirms the address is live.
Using social media
  • Know how to block and report unwanted users.
  • Be aware of the potential dangers of meeting an online contact face to face - people are not always who they claim to be. If a meeting happens, it must be in a public place with a trusted adult present.
  • Avoid the distribution of inappropriate images, of yourself or of anyone else.
  • Avoid the use of inappropriate language.
  • Respect the confidentiality of other people's personal data - do not post information or photographs of others without permission.
  • Keep privacy settings tight so only known friends can see your posts, and do not reveal your location.
Online gaming
  • Do not use real names - choose a username that gives nothing away.
  • Do not give out personal or financial data, including card details, address or school.
  • Be careful with in-game chat, and report and block abusive players.
  • Be aware that other players may not be the age they claim, and take regular breaks.
Check your understanding
Why is a photograph of a child in school uniform treated as personal data that needs protecting?
The uniform identifies the school the child attends, so anyone seeing the photograph knows where to find them. Combined with a name, this puts the child's physical safety at risk.
A learner is asked to meet an online gaming friend at the weekend. Give the advice you would offer.
Do not meet alone: people online are not always who they say they are. Tell a parent or trusted adult, and if any meeting takes place it should be in a public place with a trusted adult present. Block and report the contact if they insist on secrecy or ask for personal or financial data.
8.3

Security of Data

Threats to data

Hacking
  • What it is: gaining unauthorised access to a computer system, usually to view, steal, change or delete data
Effects
  • Data can be stolen, corrupted or deleted
  • Identity theft and fraud
  • Loss of confidentiality, and damage to the organisation's reputation
Measures that must be taken to protect data
  • Use strong passwords and change them regularly
  • Install and maintain a firewall
  • Use two-factor authentication and biometrics
  • Encrypt data so that stolen data is meaningless
  • Keep operating system and software up to date, and use up-to-date anti-malware
  • Restrict access rights so users only see the data they need
Phishing
  • What it is: the criminal sends a legitimate-looking email containing a link; clicking the link takes the user to a fake website that asks for personal or financial details
Prevention
  • Never click a link in an unexpected email - type the address yourself
  • Use an up-to-date browser and email filter that flags known phishing sites
  • Check for poor spelling, a general greeting and a mismatched sender address
  • Remember that genuine banks never ask for a password or PIN by email
Pharming
  • What it is: malicious code is installed on the user's computer or on a DNS server; it redirects the user to a fake website even when they type the correct address
  • More dangerous than phishing because the user does nothing wrong
Prevention
  • Use up-to-date anti-malware software to detect the redirecting code
  • Check the site uses https and shows a valid digital certificate before entering data
  • Use a reliable internet service provider that protects its DNS servers
  • Keep the browser and operating system patched
Smishing and vishing
  • Smishing: phishing carried out by SMS text message - the text contains a link or a number to call.
  • Vishing: phishing carried out by voice telephone call - the caller claims to be from the bank and asks for details or asks the victim to transfer money.
Prevention
  • Never phone a number given in a text - use the number printed on your card or statement
  • Do not give personal or financial details to anyone who rings you; hang up and call the bank back
  • Block and report the sender or caller, and do not reply to unexpected texts
  • Be suspicious of urgency - criminals create panic so victims act without thinking
Viruses and malware
  • What they are: a virus is program code that replicates itself and attaches to other files, deleting or corrupting data. Malware is the wider term, including worms, trojan horses, ransomware, spyware and adware.
Effects
  • Files are deleted or corrupted, and the computer runs slowly or crashes
  • Ransomware encrypts files and demands payment
  • Spyware and key logging software record everything typed, including passwords
Preventative action, especially against downloaded files
  • Install anti-virus software and keep it up to date, running regular full scans
  • Scan every downloaded file before opening it, and scan any storage media used to transfer data
  • Only download from trusted, reputable websites, and never open attachments from unknown senders
  • Use a firewall, keep software patched, and take regular backups
Card fraud
  • Shoulder surfing: the criminal watches the victim entering a PIN at an ATM or till, or listens to card details being read out. Prevention: shield the keypad with your hand, stand close to the machine, be aware of people and cameras nearby.
  • Card cloning: a skimmer fitted over the card slot copies the data from the magnetic stripe onto a blank card. Prevention: use chip and PIN or contactless rather than the stripe, check the machine for anything unusual attached, and check statements for unknown transactions.
  • Key logging: software or hardware records every key pressed, capturing card numbers and passwords. Prevention: use up-to-date anti-spyware, use a drop-down box or on-screen keyboard for parts of a password, and use two-factor authentication so a stolen password is not enough.

Passwords and authentication

Strong and weak passwords

A strong password
  • Is long - at least eight characters
  • Mixes upper case, lower case, numbers and symbols
  • Is not a word found in a dictionary and contains no personal information
  • Is different for every account
A weak password
  • Is short and all lower case
  • Is a dictionary word, a name, a birthday or "password123"
  • Is reused on several accounts
  • Is written down next to the computer

Avoiding password interception

  • Use up-to-date anti-spyware software to detect and remove key logging and spyware.
  • Change passwords regularly, so an intercepted password soon becomes useless.
  • Never send a password by email, and never enter one on a page reached from an emailed link.
  • Only enter passwords on sites using https, and log out fully when finished.

Other authentication methods

Zero login
The system identifies the user automatically from biometrics and behavioural patterns - how they type, hold the device, or walk - so no password is entered at all. Very convenient and hard to steal, but it raises privacy concerns and the user is less aware they are logged in.
Biometric methods
Fingerprint, face, iris, retina, hand or voice recognition. Cannot be forgotten, lost or shared, and are very hard to forge - but equipment is costly, injury or illness can cause false rejection, and stolen biometric data can never be changed.
Magnetic stripes and smart cards
A magnetic stripe holds a small amount of data read by swiping; a smart card holds far more data on an embedded chip, which is encrypted and much harder to clone. Both can be lost or stolen, so they are usually combined with a PIN.
Physical tokens
A small device the user carries, such as a card reader or key fob, that generates a one-time password from the current time or from a challenge. The code is only valid for a short period, so an intercepted code is useless - but the token can be lost and needs a battery.
Electronic tokens
An app on the user's smartphone generates the one-time code, or a code is sent by text. No extra device to carry, but it depends on the phone being charged, in signal and not itself compromised.

Anti-malware software

  • Anti-virus and anti-malware software checks files against a database of known malware signatures and watches for suspicious behaviour.
  • When malware is found the software removes it, or quarantines it - moving it to a safe area where it cannot run - so the user can decide whether to delete it or, if it was a false alarm, restore it.
  • The software must be kept up to date, because new malware appears constantly and out-of-date signatures will not recognise it.
  • It should scan the storage media used to transfer data, such as pen drives and portable hard drives, before files are opened.
  • It should scan data and software when downloading, before the file is run.
  • Full scans should be scheduled regularly, and real-time protection left switched on.

Methods of protecting data

Biometrics
  • Access is granted only when a physical characteristic matches a stored template.
  • Used to unlock devices, enter secure buildings and authorise payments.
  • Cannot be guessed, shared or forgotten - but data must itself be stored securely, because it can never be changed if stolen.
Digital certificate
  • Purpose: proves that a website or sender is who it claims to be, so the user knows the site is genuine.
  • Contents: the owner's name, the owner's public key, the serial number, the expiry date, and the digital signature of the certificate authority that issued it.
Secure Socket Layer (SSL)
  • A protocol that creates an encrypted link between the server and the client computer, so data sent between them cannot be understood if intercepted.
  • The browser requests identification, the server sends its digital certificate, the browser checks it, and once trusted the encrypted session begins.
  • Shown by https and a padlock in the address bar - always check for it before entering card details or passwords.
Encryption
  • Purpose: scrambles data using an encryption key so that it is meaningless to anyone who intercepts or steals it; only someone with the key can decrypt it.
  • Used to protect data on hard discs, email, data in the cloud and HTTPS websites.
  • Encryption does not stop data being stolen - it stops stolen data being understood.
Firewall
  • Purpose: sits between the computer or network and the internet and examines all incoming and outgoing traffic against a set of criteria.
  • Blocks traffic that fails the criteria, helps prevent hacking and malware, and can block access to undesirable sites.
  • Keeps a log of all traffic, and can warn the user of an attempted intrusion.
  • Can be hardware, software, or both - but it cannot stop a user who brings malware in on a pen drive, or a member of staff misusing their own access.
Two-factor authentication (2FA)
  • Purpose: requires two separate pieces of evidence of identity - typically something you know (a password) plus something you have (a one-time code sent to a phone or generated by a token).
  • Function: after the password is accepted, a code is sent or generated; the user must enter it before access is granted.
  • A stolen password on its own is therefore useless to a criminal.
User id and password
  • The user id identifies who is logging on; the password proves it is really them.
  • Together they restrict access to authorised users only, and allow different access rights to be given to different users.
  • They create an audit trail showing who accessed what and when.
  • The system can lock the account after a set number of failed attempts, blocking automated guessing.
Check your understanding
Explain the difference between phishing and pharming.
In phishing the user is sent a legitimate-looking email with a link, and is taken to a fake website only if they click it. In pharming, malicious code on the user's computer or on a DNS server redirects them to the fake site even when they type the correct address themselves, so they can be caught without doing anything wrong.
A company already uses passwords. Explain how two-factor authentication improves security.
2FA requires a second, separate piece of evidence as well as the password - usually a one-time code sent to the user's phone or produced by a token. If a criminal steals or intercepts the password, it is not enough on its own, because they would also need the user's physical device.
Encryption does not stop data being stolen. Explain why it is still worth using.
Encryption scrambles the data with a key so that anyone who intercepts or steals it cannot understand it without the decryption key. The data is therefore useless to the thief, protecting confidentiality even though the theft itself was not prevented.
