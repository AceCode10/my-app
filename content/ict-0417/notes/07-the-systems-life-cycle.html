Cambridge IGCSE ICT 0417 · Topic 7

The Systems Life Cycle

The systems life cycle is the ordered set of stages an organisation follows when it replaces an existing system with a new computer system: analysis → design → development and testing → implementation → documentation → evaluation. Learn the stages in order, and learn what is produced at each one.

Syllabus coverage: 7.1 Analysis · 7.2 Design · 7.3 Development and testing · 7.4 Implementation · 7.5 Documentation · 7.6 Evaluation
7.1

Analysis

Research methods for analysing the current system

Observation
  • Characteristics: the analyst watches the existing system in operation, following the flow of data from start to finish
Uses
  • Seeing what really happens rather than what people say happens
  • Timing how long each task takes and spotting bottlenecks
Advantages
  • Gives reliable, first-hand data about the whole system
  • Relatively cheap - only the analyst's time is needed
  • Shows problems staff may not mention or may not notice
Disadvantages
  • Staff may work differently because they are being watched (the Hawthorne effect)
  • Time-consuming, and a busy or quiet day may not be typical
  • Staff may object to being observed
Interviews
  • Characteristics: the analyst questions individuals face to face, usually one at a time, and can follow up interesting answers
Uses
  • Finding out in depth what managers and staff need from the new system
  • Exploring complex problems that a questionnaire could not capture
Advantages
  • Questions can be adapted and followed up as the interview goes on
  • The interviewee can explain their answers fully, giving detailed information
  • Body language and tone add extra information, and misunderstandings can be cleared up at once
Disadvantages
  • Very time-consuming and expensive - only a few people can be interviewed
  • Answers cannot be given anonymously, so staff may not be honest
  • Both people must be free at the same time, and answers are harder to analyse statistically
Questionnaires
  • Characteristics: a set of written questions, often with tick boxes, given to many people at once and returned for analysis
Uses
  • Collecting opinions from a large number of staff or customers
  • Gathering data that can be counted and compared
Advantages
  • Many people can be asked quickly and cheaply
  • Answers can be anonymous, so people are more honest
  • People answer in their own time, and tick-box answers are quick to analyse, often automatically by OMR
Disadvantages
  • Often a low response rate, and those who reply may not be typical
  • Questions cannot be followed up, and answers cannot be expanded
  • Questions may be misunderstood, and people may not answer truthfully
Examination of existing documents
  • Characteristics: the analyst studies the paperwork the current system uses and produces - forms, invoices, reports, files and manuals
Uses
  • Finding the exact data items, field sizes and formats the system handles
  • Seeing the volume of data processed and the layout of existing reports
Advantages
  • Shows exactly what data is used, in what format and in what quantity
  • Can be done without disturbing staff, and gives an accurate picture of the volume of work
Disadvantages
  • Time-consuming and can be expensive if there are many documents
  • Documents may be out of date or incomplete
  • Does not show how people actually work around the paperwork

Recording and analysing information about the current system

Whatever methods are used, the analyst must identify:

  • The inputs of the current system - what data goes in, where it comes from and in what format
  • The outputs - the reports, screens and documents the system produces, and who uses them
  • The processing - the calculations, searches, sorting and decisions carried out on the data
  • The problems with the current system - what is slow, inaccurate, expensive or unreliable
  • The user requirements - what the people using the system need to be able to do
  • The information requirements - what information the new system must produce

System specification

The analyst must identify and justify suitable hardware and software for the new system - it is never enough to list items, you must say why each is needed.

Hardware, with justification
  • Input devices chosen for the data - for example a bar code reader because stock must be entered quickly and accurately
  • Output devices chosen for the output - for example a laser printer because hundreds of invoices are printed daily
  • Storage of a stated capacity because of the volume of records and backups
  • Processor and memory sufficient because of the number of users and the size of the data
  • Network hardware because several branches must share the data
Software, with justification
  • Operating system suited to the hardware and the number of users
  • Database software because large volumes of related records must be searched and sorted
  • Spreadsheet software because calculations and 'what if' modelling are needed
  • Word processing or desktop publishing because letters and reports must be produced
  • Security and utility software - anti-malware, backup, compression
Check your understanding
An analyst needs the honest opinions of 400 employees. Which research method should be used and why?
A questionnaire. It can reach all 400 people quickly and cheaply, the answers can be given anonymously so people are more honest, and tick-box responses are fast to analyse. Interviews would take far too long and would not be anonymous.
Give one disadvantage of observation that interviews do not have.
Staff may change the way they work because they know they are being watched, so what the analyst sees is not typical. In an interview the analyst can also ask follow-up questions, which observation cannot do.
7.2

Design

File and data structures

The designer decides, for every item of data to be stored:

  • Field name - a short, meaningful name for each item, for example DateOfBirth
  • Data type - text, numeric (integer, decimal, currency), date/time or Boolean/logical
  • Field length - how many characters are allowed, chosen so that no data is cut off but no space is wasted
  • Coding of data - shortening data to a code, for example M for male and F for female, or S, M, L for sizes
  • Which field is the primary key, and which fields link tables together
Why code data?
  • Less storage space is used
  • Faster to type in and faster to search and sort
  • Fewer typing errors, and easier to validate
Drawbacks of coding
  • The user must learn what the codes mean
  • Coarse codes can lose detail
  • A mistyped code may still be a valid code, so the error is not detected

Validation routines

Validation is an automatic check, carried out by the computer, that data entered is reasonable and of the right type. It cannot check that the data is correct - only that it is sensible.
CheckWhat it doesExample
Range checkData must lie between an upper and a lower limitA month must be between 1 and 12
Character checkData must contain only permitted charactersA name must not contain digits
Length checkData must have an exact or a maximum number of charactersA password must be at least 8 characters
Type checkData must be of the correct data typeAge must be numeric
Format checkData must follow a stated pattern of charactersA date entered as dd/mm/yyyy
Presence checkThe field must not be left emptyAn email address must be entered on a form
Check digitAn extra digit calculated from the other digits and added to the end; recalculated on entry to detect errorsThe last digit of a bar code or ISBN
Exam tip: a check digit catches transposed digits - for example 1243 typed instead of 1234 - which a range or type check would happily accept.

Input and output formats

Input formats - data capture forms
  • Clear title and simple instructions at the top
  • Enough space for each item, with boxes for individual characters where needed
  • Fields in the same order as the source document, so entry is fast
  • Tick boxes, radio buttons and drop-down lists to limit choices and reduce errors
  • Sensible use of white space, consistent fonts, and no unnecessary questions
Output formats - screen and report layouts
  • Clear report title, headers and footers, and page numbering
  • All required data and labels displayed in full, with nothing truncated
  • Numeric data right-aligned or decimal-aligned, with the correct decimal places and currency symbols
  • Tabular or columnar layout chosen to suit the data
  • Only the information the user actually needs, in a logical order
Check your understanding
A field stores a percentage mark. State two validation checks and why each is needed.
A type check makes sure the entry is numeric, so letters cannot be entered. A range check makes sure it is between 0 and 100, so an impossible mark such as 150 is rejected. A presence check would also stop the field being left blank.
Why can a validation check not guarantee that data is correct?
Validation only tests that data is reasonable. A pupil aged 14 entered as 15 passes every check, because 15 is a valid, sensible age - it is simply the wrong value. Only verification against the original source can catch that.
7.3

Development and Testing

Why the system must be tested before implementation

  • To find and correct errors before the system is used for real work
  • To make sure the system meets the requirements identified in the analysis
  • To prevent loss of data, loss of money and loss of confidence in the system
  • Because errors found after implementation are far more expensive and damaging to fix

Test designs and test strategies

Test designs - what is tested
  • Data structures - are the field names, types and lengths correct?
  • File structures - are records stored, retrieved and linked correctly?
  • Input formats - do the data capture forms work and accept the right data?
  • Output formats - are screens and reports laid out correctly and complete?
  • Validation routines - does each check reject bad data and accept good data?
Test strategies - how it is tested
  • Test each module on its own as it is written
  • Test each function within a module
  • Test the whole system once the modules are joined together
  • Re-test after every correction, because a fix can break something else

Types of test data

TypeDefinitionExpected outcomeExample (age 11 to 18)
NormalData that is within the acceptable range and of the correct typeAccepted14
AbnormalData outside the acceptable range, of the wrong type, or missingRejected, with an error message25, "fourteen", a blank entry
ExtremeData at the very edges of the acceptable range - the largest and smallest valid valuesAccepted11 and 18
Do not confuse extreme with abnormal. Extreme data is valid and must be accepted; abnormal data is invalid and must be rejected. Some questions also use boundary data, meaning the values just inside and just outside each limit.

The test plan

A test plan is a table, completed as testing goes on. Its columns are:

  • Test data - the exact value to be entered
  • Expected outcome - what should happen, decided before the test is run
  • Actual outcome - what really happened
  • Remedial action - what was changed if the two did not match, and the re-test result

The use of live data

  • Live data is real data taken from the existing system, whose correct results are already known.
  • It is used once testing with test data is complete, to check the new system against known outcomes.
  • It tests the system with realistic volumes, and shows whether it is fast enough in practice.
  • Users can compare the new results with the old ones, which builds confidence in the system.
  • A copy of the live data is always used, so that real records cannot be damaged.
Check your understanding
A field accepts marks from 0 to 60. Give one item of normal, abnormal and extreme test data, and the expected outcome of each.
Normal: 35 - accepted. Abnormal: 75 or "forty" or a blank - rejected with an error message. Extreme: 0 and 60 - accepted, because they are the smallest and largest valid values.
Why is live data used as well as test data?
Live data is real data whose correct results are already known, so the new system's output can be compared with the old. It also tests the system with realistic volumes of data, showing whether performance is acceptable, and it gives users confidence in the new system.
7.4

Implementation

The four methods of implementation

Direct changeover
  • Characteristics: the old system is stopped and the new system starts immediately, usually overnight or over a weekend
Uses
  • Small systems, or where the old system has already failed
  • Where the new system must be in place by a fixed date
Advantages
  • The cheapest and fastest method - only one system is run
  • The benefits of the new system are available immediately
  • No need to enter data twice or pay staff to run two systems
Disadvantages
  • The most risky - if the new system fails there is nothing to fall back on
  • Data can be lost and the business can stop trading
  • Staff must be fully trained before the changeover
Parallel running
  • Characteristics: the old and new systems are run side by side on the same data until the new one is trusted
Uses
  • Systems where failure would be very serious, such as payroll or banking
Advantages
  • If the new system fails, the old system is still running, so nothing is lost
  • Results from the two systems can be compared to check the new one is correct
  • Staff can be trained gradually on the new system
Disadvantages
  • The most expensive method - two systems and extra staff
  • All data has to be entered twice, which takes time and can introduce errors
  • Takes much longer to complete the changeover
Pilot running
  • Characteristics: the whole new system is introduced into one branch, department or store first; when it works, it is rolled out everywhere
Uses
  • Organisations with many similar sites, such as a supermarket chain or a bank
Advantages
  • If the system fails, only one part of the organisation is affected
  • Faults are found and fixed before the full roll-out
  • Staff from the pilot site can train the others
  • Much cheaper than parallel running
Disadvantages
  • Takes longer than a direct changeover
  • The pilot branch has no fallback if the system fails there
  • The pilot site may not be typical of all sites
Phased implementation
  • Characteristics: one part of the new system at a time replaces the equivalent part of the old system; the next part follows only when the first works
Uses
  • Large systems made of clearly separate modules, for example stock control then payroll then accounts
Advantages
  • If a phase fails, only that part of the system is affected and the rest of the old system still runs
  • Staff have time to learn each part before the next arrives
  • Problems are found in a controlled way, phase by phase
Disadvantages
  • Takes the longest time overall
  • More expensive than a direct changeover
  • The old and new parts must be able to work together, which can be complicated

Choosing the right method

MethodCostRiskBest when
Direct changeoverLowestHighestThe system is small and simple, or the old one has already failed
Parallel runningHighestLowestFailure would be disastrous, such as payroll or patient records
Pilot runningModerateLimited to one siteThe organisation has many similar branches or stores
Phased implementationModerateLimited to one moduleThe system divides naturally into separate parts
Check your understanding
A hospital is introducing a new patient records system. Which implementation method should it use, and why?
Parallel running. Patient records are safety-critical, so if the new system failed the old one would still be available and no records would be lost. The results of the two systems can also be compared to prove the new one is correct. The drawbacks - high cost and entering data twice - are accepted because of the risk to patients.
State one advantage of pilot running over parallel running.
Pilot running is much cheaper, because only one branch runs the new system and data does not have to be entered twice everywhere. Staff from the pilot branch can also train staff at the other sites.
7.5

Documentation

Purpose and uses

Technical documentation
  • Written for programmers and technicians
  • Used to maintain, repair, update and improve the system in the future
  • Assumes technical knowledge
User documentation
  • Written for the people who will use the system
  • Used to learn how to operate the system and to solve everyday problems
  • Written in plain language, with screenshots and no jargon

Components of each type

Technical documentation contains
  • Purpose of the system/program
  • Limitations of the system
  • Program listing
  • Program language
  • Program flowcharts / algorithms
  • System flowcharts
  • Hardware and software requirements
  • File structures
  • List of variables
  • Input format
  • Output format
  • Sample runs / test runs
  • Validation routines
User documentation contains
  • Purpose of the system
  • Limitations of the system
  • Hardware and software requirements
  • How to load / run / install the software
  • How to save a file
  • How to print data
  • How to add records
  • How to delete / edit records
  • Input format
  • Output format
  • Sample runs
  • Error messages
  • Error handling
  • Troubleshooting guide / helpline
  • Frequently asked questions
  • Glossary of terms
Shared items: purpose of the system, limitations of the system, hardware and software requirements, input format, output format and sample runs appear in both documents. Everything about program code, flowcharts, variables and validation is technical only; everything about how to carry out tasks, error messages and FAQs is user only.
Check your understanding
Which document would contain a list of variables, and why?
Technical documentation, because a list of variables is only useful to a programmer who needs to understand or modify the code. A user would have no use for it.
Name three items that appear in both technical and user documentation.
Purpose of the system, limitations of the system, hardware and software requirements, input format, output format, sample runs - any three.
7.6

Evaluation

How a solution is evaluated

Evaluation happens after implementation. It judges the finished system against what was asked for, using three headings:

  • Efficiency of the solution - is the system fast enough? Does it use resources sensibly? Does it produce results more quickly and at lower cost than the old system?
  • Ease of use of the solution - can users carry out tasks without difficulty? Are the screens clear, is the documentation adequate, and how much training was needed?
  • Appropriateness of the solution - does it actually do the job the organisation needs, on the hardware it has, for the users it has?

The steps of an evaluation

  1. Compare the solution with the original task requirements, requirement by requirement, and state whether each has been met.
  2. Identify any limitations - things the system cannot do, or does not do well enough.
  3. Identify necessary improvements to the system, and say how they would be made.
  4. Evaluate the users' responses to the results of testing the system - gathered through questionnaires, interviews and observation of them working.
Exam tip: an evaluation answer must contain a judgement. "The system produces reports in 3 seconds compared with 2 hours by hand, so it is far more efficient" earns marks; "the system works" does not.
Check your understanding
Describe two ways the developer could collect users' responses to the new system.
Questionnaires given to all users, which can be anonymous and reach many people quickly, and interviews with a smaller number of users, where answers can be explored in depth. Observing users working with the system also shows where they struggle.
Put the six stages of the systems life cycle in order.
Analysis → Design → Development and testing → Implementation → Documentation → Evaluation. Evaluation may lead back to analysis, which is why it is called a cycle.
