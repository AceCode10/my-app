Communication

The final theory topic covers the two tools you will use every day: email and the internet. Expect questions on netiquette and email constraints, on the difference between the internet and the World Wide Web, and on judging how reliable information found online really is.

Syllabus coverage: 10.1 Communication with other ICT users using email · 10.2 Effective use of the internet
10.1

Communication Using Email

Characteristics and uses of email

  • Messages are sent electronically between named addresses and arrive within seconds, anywhere in the world.
  • Files of almost any type can be sent as attachments.
  • Messages are stored, so there is a permanent, searchable record of what was sent and when.
  • One message can be sent to many recipients at once, and replies keep the conversation together.
Carbon copy (cc)
Sends a copy to additional recipients. Every recipient can see every address in the To and cc fields. Use it when the recipients know each other and should see who else received the message.
Blind carbon copy (bcc)
Sends a copy without the other recipients seeing that address. Use it when the recipients do not know each other, so that their email addresses stay private - this protects personal data and reduces spam.
Forward
Sends a message you received on to somebody else, with any attachments. Check first whether the original sender would be happy for it to be passed on.
Attachments
Files sent with the message. There is usually a size limit, so large files should be compressed (zipped) or shared through cloud storage. Attachments from unknown senders are the commonest way of spreading malware, so they must be scanned before opening.
Email groups
A single named list containing many addresses, so one message reaches the whole group. Groups save time, avoid missing anyone out, and make it easy to keep mailing lists up to date - for example a class, a department or all customers.

Constraints on email use

Acceptable language
  • No obscene, abusive, racist or discriminatory language
  • No language that could be read as harassment or bullying
  • Avoid capital letters throughout, which reads as shouting
  • Remember that tone is easily misread in writing, and sarcasm rarely travels well
Guidelines set by an employer
  • A company email policy states what may and may not be sent from a work account
  • It may forbid personal use, large attachments or sending confidential data outside the company
  • It often requires a standard signature and house style
  • Employers may legally monitor work email, and staff can be dismissed for breaching the policy
The need for security
  • Use strong passwords and two-factor authentication on the account
  • Encrypt confidential messages and attachments
  • Do not open attachments or links from unknown senders - they may be phishing or malware
  • Use bcc for group mailings so addresses are not disclosed
  • Log out on shared computers, and never send passwords or card details by email
Copyright
  • Material received by email is still protected by copyright
  • Do not forward or publish copyrighted text, images or files without permission
  • Attaching a copyrighted file to a message counts as copying it

Netiquette

Netiquette - "internet etiquette" - is the set of accepted rules of courtesy when communicating electronically.
  • Use a clear, meaningful subject line so the reader knows what the message is about.
  • Keep messages concise and to the point, and check spelling and grammar before sending.
  • Do not type in capital letters - it is read as shouting.
  • Do not use abusive, offensive or discriminatory language, and do not flame or troll.
  • Respect other people's privacy - do not forward private messages or disclose addresses; use bcc for group mail.
  • Do not send or forward spam or chain letters.
  • Reply within a reasonable time, and only to the people who need the reply - avoid "reply all" without reason.
  • Check attachments are not too large, and say in the message what has been attached.
  • Do not use jargon, abbreviations or emoticons in formal messages.
  • Re-read a message before sending it - once sent it cannot be recalled.

Spam email

Spam is unwanted, unsolicited email sent in bulk to very many addresses, usually advertising or attempting fraud.

Effects of spam

  • Wastes the recipient's time reading and deleting it, and important messages are missed among it.
  • Fills the mailbox and uses up storage space and bandwidth, slowing the network.
  • Often carries malware in attachments or links, or is used for phishing.
  • Can contain offensive or inappropriate material.

Methods of preventing spam

  • Use a spam filter and keep it up to date; mark unwanted mail as spam so the filter learns.
  • Never reply to spam or click "unsubscribe" in it - that confirms the address is live.
  • Do not publish your email address on websites, forums or social media.
  • Use bcc when mailing a group, so addresses are not passed on.
  • Use a second, disposable address when signing up to websites.
  • Block the sender, and read the privacy policy before giving an address to a company.
  • Keep anti-malware software up to date, in case spam carries an infected attachment.
Check your understanding
A club secretary emails 200 members about a meeting. Explain why bcc should be used instead of cc.
With cc, every recipient would see all 200 email addresses, which discloses members' personal data without their consent and exposes them to spam if any account is compromised. bcc hides the addresses from the other recipients.
Give three rules of netiquette.
Any three of: use a clear subject line; keep messages concise and check spelling; do not type in capitals, as it reads as shouting; do not use abusive or offensive language; respect privacy and do not forward private messages; do not send spam or chain letters.
10.2

Effective Use of the Internet

The internet, intranet, extranet and the World Wide Web

TermWhat it isWho can use it
InternetA worldwide network of interconnected networks - the infrastructure itselfAnyone with a connection, worldwide
World Wide Web (WWW)The collection of web pages and websites that is carried on the internet, accessed using HTTP and a browserAnyone on the internet
IntranetA private network inside one organisation, using internet technologies; sits behind a firewallEmployees or members of that organisation only
ExtranetAn intranet that has been partly extended to selected outsiders, reached over the internet with a loginEmployees plus named customers, suppliers or partners
The one-line distinction examiners want: the internet is the network; the World Wide Web is the information - the pages - carried on it. Email, file transfer and streaming also use the internet but are not part of the web.

Why an organisation uses an intranet

  • Information can only be seen by authorised staff, which is far more secure than a public website
  • Content is written by and for the organisation, so it is relevant and can be trusted
  • The organisation controls what can be viewed, and can block undesirable sites
  • Less risk of hacking and of external attack, because it sits behind a firewall

Blogs, forums, wikis and social networking

Blog
  • A personal online journal, in reverse date order
  • Written and controlled by one author; readers may comment but cannot edit
  • Used for opinion, news and product reviews
Forum
  • An online discussion site where members post messages in threads
  • Usually overseen by a moderator who can remove unsuitable posts
  • Used for support, hobbies and technical help
Wiki
  • A website that many users can edit, adding and changing content directly
  • Keeps a history of every edit so changes can be undone
  • Builds up large collaborative reference works - but anyone can post something wrong, so it must be checked
Social networking
  • Users create a profile and share posts, photographs and messages with a network of contacts
  • Content is controlled by privacy settings; users choose who can see what
  • Used for keeping in touch, groups, advertising and campaigns - with eSafety risks attached

Functionality

Internet Service Provider (ISP)
The company that provides the connection to the internet. It supplies the line and router, an IP address, usually email accounts and web space, and may also filter content and provide security services. A subscription is normally paid.
Structure of a web address (URL)
A Uniform Resource Locator gives the unique address of a resource, in three parts:
protocol (https://) + domain name / host (www.example.com) + path and file name (/notes/ict/topic10.html). The domain itself shows the type of organisation (.com, .org, .gov, .ac) and often the country (.uk, .ke, .sg).
Hyperlink
Text or an image that, when clicked, jumps to another location - a bookmark on the same page, another page on the same site, another website, or an email address. Hyperlinks are what make the web navigable rather than linear.
Web browser
Software that requests, interprets and displays web pages. It renders the HTML, applies the CSS, runs scripts, stores bookmarks/favourites and history, keeps cookies, and shows whether a connection is secure.

Using a search engine

Advantages
  • Very fast searching - millions of pages are indexed and searched in a fraction of a second
  • A huge amount of information is available on any topic, from around the world, free of charge
  • Available 24 hours a day from any connected device
  • Results can be narrowed with quotation marks, +/- and advanced options
Disadvantages
  • So many results are returned that finding the relevant information takes time
  • It is difficult to judge which sources are reliable, as anyone can publish
  • Results may be biased by advertising and by the ranking algorithm
  • Information may be out of date, incomplete or simply wrong
  • Users may not use effective keywords, and may be distracted by unrelated content

Searching effectively

  • Choose specific keywords rather than a whole question, and avoid very common words.
  • Put a phrase in "quotation marks" to search for those words together.
  • Use + / - or AND, OR and NOT to include and exclude terms.
  • Restrict the search to a domain or file type, or use the advanced search options and date filters.

Evaluating information found on the internet

Anyone can publish anything, so every source must be judged on four things:

How up to date is it?
  • Look for the date the page was written or last updated
  • Check whether the links still work - broken links suggest a neglected site
  • Compare with the most recent information elsewhere
How reliable is it?
  • Who wrote it, and what are their qualifications?
  • Is the site run by a respected organisation - a government, university or established company?
  • Are sources and references given, and is the spelling and grammar accurate?
  • Is the same information found on other independent sites?
Is it biased?
  • Does the author gain from the reader believing it - is it trying to sell something?
  • Is only one side of the argument presented?
  • Is the site funded or sponsored by an interested party?
  • Is the language emotive rather than factual?
Is it valid?
  • Does it actually answer the question being asked?
  • Is the evidence and reasoning sound, and can it be checked?
  • Does it agree with other trusted sources?

Internet protocols

ProtocolFull nameWhat it does
HTTPHyperText Transfer ProtocolThe set of rules for transferring web pages between a web server and a browser. Data is sent as plain text.
HTTPSHyperText Transfer Protocol secure variantHTTP with the data encrypted. Shown by the padlock in the address bar; must be used before entering passwords or card details.
FTPFile Transfer ProtocolThe rules for transferring files between computers over a network, for example uploading a website to a web server.
SSLSecure Socket LayerThe protocol that creates the encrypted link between the server and the client, using a digital certificate. It is what puts the "S" in HTTPS.

Risks of using the internet, and controlling access

Risks

  • Inappropriate material - violent, pornographic or extremist content reaching children
  • Criminal material - fraud, illegal downloads, sites promoting crime or terrorism
  • Phishing, pharming, hacking and malware
  • Cyberbullying, grooming and online scams
  • Unreliable or deliberately false information
  • Addiction to gaming or social media, and excessive screen time

Restricting data

Parental control
  • Software that blocks categories of website and restricts the hours of use
  • Can limit which apps are installed and report on activity
  • Set up and protected by a password only the parent knows
Educational control
  • The school's network filters block unsuitable sites and social media
  • Only approved, trusted websites are available, and an acceptable use policy must be signed
  • Activity is logged and monitored by staff
ISP control
  • Filters applied at the provider level, covering every device on the connection
  • Known illegal and harmful sites are blocked before they reach the home
  • Optional family-friendly filtering can be switched on by the account holder
Check your understanding
State the difference between the internet and the World Wide Web.
The internet is the worldwide network of interconnected networks - the infrastructure. The World Wide Web is the collection of web pages and websites carried on that infrastructure and viewed with a browser. Email and file transfer use the internet but are not part of the web.
A student finds a web page claiming a food cures a disease. Give three checks on its reliability.
Check who wrote it and whether they are qualified, and whether the site belongs to a respected organisation such as a university or health service. Check when it was last updated and whether its links still work. Check for bias - is the site selling that food? - and see whether other independent, trusted sources say the same thing.
Why must HTTPS be used when paying for goods online?
HTTPS uses SSL to encrypt the data passing between the browser and the server, so card and personal details cannot be understood if they are intercepted. Plain HTTP sends the data as readable text.
